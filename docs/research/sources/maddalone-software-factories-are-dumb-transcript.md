## [S2] Metaphors shape mental models, which shape habits

Because we have a tendency to understand new technology by comparing it to the world we already know. Your car's engine power is probably measured in horsepower, which is a clear throwback to the dominant mode of transportation before automobiles, which was horses. And today, we still pretend that computers are little desks. You know, they have documents and file cabinets and address books and a little trash can and a clipboard because it's a solid metaphor that helps people. Take the universal save icon. It's a three and a half inch floppy disk. And for an entire generation of computer users, that totally made sense. They knew it was an image of a floppy disk, and they knew what it meant to save something to a floppy disk. But there are adults today who have never seen a floppy disk in person, and they recognize that icon not because it represents something that they've physically experienced. They've just learned what it means, the intent and meaning behind it. So the metaphor has outlived the thing that it's supposed to represent.

And these metaphors work. They make technology more approachable by borrowing from the world people already understood. But they also shaped what people came to expect from computers. We built interfaces around these metaphors and developed habits around those interfaces and eventually started treating those habits as though they were inherent properties of computing itself. But they're not fundamental properties of computing. They're design decisions inherited from a time when we needed to make computers more understandable to a person who had never used one. So in short, too late, a metaphor like desktop, a great metaphor, shapes a mental model. And that mental model shapes habits. And when a mental model is correct, the habits are correct. And likewise, when a mental model is wrong, the habits are wrong. When we rely on training wheels, we may forget eventually that the training wheels are removable. And you may find yourself defending design decisions made in the 1970s or the 1980s as though they were laws of physics.

## [S3] Best practices came from the cost of human-written code

A lot of our best and good software engineering practices are derived from the cost of developing, maintaining, and coordinating software written by human beings. But AI is fundamentally changing the economics of software development, which should mean that some of our best practices should stop making sense. However, whenever I look at a so-called Vibe-coded app, I see the exact same best practices being implemented. And to me, that's a signal that we're not quite ready to let go of those old mental models, me included.

## [S4] Craftsman to urban planner; "design engineering"

So take a book like The Pragmatic Programmer. The entire philosophy, and honestly, the whole tradition of modern software development, is based on the software developer as craftsman. You know, hand polishing every single function, obsessing over clean code, and adhering to strict policies like D-R-Y. Don't repeat yourself. And that mindset made total sense when every single character on the screen was typed by a human hand, and every future bug meant that a human had to parse that exact code with their human eyes. But if AI can refactor or discard, you know, 500 lines of boilerplate in four seconds, it changes the cost of duplication. It changes the cost of abstraction. So suddenly spending three hours, you know, hand polishing your pristine hypermodule class hierarchy isn't craft. It's nostalgia.

We aren't woodworkers anymore. We're more akin to urban planners. An urban planner doesn't care about the precise grain of wood on a park bench. They care about traffic flow and utility capacity. And what they do is they set boundaries, they enforce constraints, and they direct growth. When your tool writes code for you, your value as an engineer is no longer your ability to write clean syntax. It's your ability to express clear intent, to evaluate trade-offs, and to guide growth. The term for this is currently design engineering. And if you are still treating software development like you're hand carving a piece of wood, you are focused on a bench while the city around you burns.

## [S5] Folders are a spatial crutch; models work semantically

Which brings us back to those metaphors that we can't seem to let go of, beginning with how we organize our work. For decades, we have kind of pretended that our software lives in physical file cabinets. We create directories and subdirectories and nested paths, all in support of a mental model based on spatial organization. The idea that in order to find something or to understand how a system works, you need to know where it lives in some sort of grid. But large language models and vector databases don't give a shit that your helper function is buried three levels deep in a folder called utils. They evaluate code based on semantic context. What the code actually means, what it does, and how it relates to the problem at hand, regardless of where it's physically located on a disk. And despite me seeing a lot of engineers say, I don't read the code anymore, it sure seems like they spend a lot of energy teaching AI to organize that code based on a crutch which we invented in 1985 to solve our very human short-term memory problem. We simply have not transitioned from the spatial organization mental model to the new semantic one. We still treat our digital world like a bunch of paper documents in a filing cabinet.

## [S6] "Senior engineers are using AI wrong"

All right, here is where I am going to lose a lot of you. So I'll just say up front, sorry, not sorry. If you pay attention, if you listen to the discourse around AI and coding, there's a narrative that's taking over. And it says that AI tools are only useful if you're already a senior software developer. The argument goes that you need an extensive background in software development in order to catch the AI's mistakes. And you need to know every classic software development pattern in order to keep the system on the rails. And right now, these are absolutely the loudest voices in the room. Senior engineers looking at these tools and saying, look how much faster I can execute the things I already know. And on the surface, it kind of sort of seems logical, but it's a trap.

When a seasoned developer uses AI, they tend to use it as a hyper-efficient autocomplete for their own mental models. They write the exact same design patterns and architectures they've been writing since 2008, only faster. They don't reimagine the work. They just supercharge the baggage and habits they bring to the work. The people who are going to move us into the next software development era are not walking around with 20 years of best practices stuck in their heads. They are the ones who don't know what a floppy disk is and they're not emotionally attached to DRY or traditional refactoring techniques. They don't have to spend a lot of energy unlearning 40 years of software development dogma and they're not trying to make AI fit an old workflow. They are completely free to invent brand new ways to work with code from scratch.

## [S7] The software factory trap

And that brings us to the most recent trap that we're all about to fall into, the software factory. So for about 20 years or so, give or take, the software industry has been sort of obsessed with converting the software development process into an assembly line process. And we have succeeded in half of that. CICD works. It's very much like an assembly line or a factory. We don't really worry that much once it's set up. The testing, building, and deployment of an application works great. But we are currently looking at a new dangerous leap into making the entire software development life cycle an assembly line process. And that assembly line would be populated by AI agents who are trained on old mental models. You can imagine some sort of input where a product manager drops a specification and an AI assembly line just starts spitting out code and on the other side out rolls a perfectly formed product or application. And that's a very attractive mental model if you believe that the modern developer's edge is speed of implementation. It's also completely wrong. The modern developer's edge isn't speed. It's taste and verification and edge case evaluation.

First, a factory assumes that design is solved, but figuring out what to build and how it should behave is the work. A ticket is not a blueprint. Second, the process of developing software changes our understanding of the specification while we're building it. It's not spec in and software out. Design engineering evolves a specification as we learn more about the problem. Third, an assembly line is meant to eliminate variations. Design engineering benefits from it. And the ambiguity doesn't just disappear. The agents will fill the gap with best guesses and very confidently produce the wrong thing.

## [S9] Close

The problem with the software factory is not that the technology is bad. It's that we're applying a 19th century industrial mental model to a 21st century cognitive shift. And just like Marvin Minsky couldn't see past his assumptions about how machines would learn, we risk missing the real power of these tools because we're too busy making them act like faster assembly line workers. We're going to use groundbreaking technology to just do the wrong things faster. The classics, they got us here. They gave us the mental models and the metaphors that we needed to survive the first era of software development. But as these new tools rewrite the baseline of what it means to build software, hanging on to those old metaphors is not going to make us better engineers.
